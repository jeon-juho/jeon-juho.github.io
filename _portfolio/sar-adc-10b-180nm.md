* **Context**: Final Year Thesis, HKUST — advisor Prof. Yihan Zhang
* **Technology**: TSMC 180 nm CMOS, 1.8 V supply
* **Verification**: Schematic-level simulation in Cadence Virtuoso
* **Recognition**: First Runner-Up, ECE Best Final Year Project/Thesis Award, Jun 2024
* **My role**: Sole author — every block below was designed and verified independently

## Goal

Build a 10-bit SAR ADC from MOS transistors and on-chip capacitors, in a commercial CMOS process
with commercial CAD tools, and understand the trade-offs between resolution, sampling rate and
power well enough to make deliberate choices about them.

The design follows the **monotonic switching** procedure: the common-mode voltage moves in a single
direction, which speeds up DAC settling, removes the need for capacitor switching in the first
comparison, and halves the number of unit capacitors needed for a given resolution. At 10 bits, that
scheme needs about **81 % less average switching energy** than conventional switching. Linearity of
the sample-and-hold comes from a **bootstrapped switch**, which fixes V<sub>GS</sub> and therefore
keeps the switch on-resistance constant across the input swing.

## Capacitive DAC

The capacitor network doubles as the sample-and-hold capacitor and as the DAC during conversion. Ten
binary-distributed capacitors are used, with C<sub>9</sub> = C<sub>10</sub> = C<sub>U</sub>, giving
C<sub>total</sub> = 2<sup>9</sup>C<sub>U</sub>. The unit capacitance is 6.93 fF — the smallest MOM
capacitor the process allows — chosen to keep the RC time constant and DAC settling time down; total
network capacitance is 3.55 pF.

## Bootstrapped sampling switch

A single-transistor switch has a gate-source voltage that tracks the input, so its on-resistance
moves with the signal and distorts the sample. Bootstrapping pins V<sub>GS</sub> to a constant,
holding on-resistance flat and improving linearity.

## Dynamic comparator

Three inputs (VP, VN, CLKC) and two outputs (VOP, VON). M8 is a biased transistor forming a current
source from the bias generated by M9; holding it in saturation reduces the effect of the
comparator's dynamic offset on conversion linearity.

## SAR logic

The logic splits in two. **Asynchronous control** takes the input clock, sampling clock and VDD and
generates the ten capacitor-control clocks (CLK1–CLK10) plus the comparator control CLKC. **DAC
control logic** combines those clocks with the comparator output to drive each capacitor's bottom
plate to either V<sub>ref</sub> or ground. Nine DAC control blocks are needed per network; the first
six switch buffers are scaled to their capacitors and the last three are unit sized, so every branch
keeps the same RC time constant.

The D flip-flops, asynchronous D flip-flops and logic gates underneath were all built as custom
cells.

## Full converter and measurement

Two bootstrapped switches sample VIP and VIN, two capacitive networks feed VP and VN into the
dynamic comparator, and the SAR logic closes the loop back onto the DAC bottom plates. Performance
was extracted by single-tone testing: differential sine inputs, output bits fed to an ideal DAC, and
a 1024-point FFT at f<sub>s</sub> = 2 MS/s with f<sub>in</sub> = 99.4 kHz.

## Results

| Specification | This work | Reference design (JSSC'10) |
|---|---|---|
| Technology | 180 nm | 130 nm |
| Supply voltage | 1.8 V | 1.2 V |
| Input CM voltage | 0.9 V | 0.6 V |
| Input range | 1.8 V<sub>p-p</sub> | 2 V<sub>p-p</sub> |
| Sampling capacitance | 3.55 pF | 2.5 pF |
| Sampling rate | 2 MS/s | 50 MS/s |
| Resolution | 10 bits | 10 bits |
| **ENOB** | **9.09 bits** | 9.18 bits |
| SNR | 56.77 dB | 54.4 dB |
| SFDR | 67.79 dBc | 61.8 dBc |
| Power | 1.920 mW | 0.826 mW |

Static accuracy lands close to the reference design — ENOB, SNR and SFDR are all comparable. Speed
and power are where the gap sits, and the older process and higher supply account for a good part of
it.

## What I would do differently

The reference design uses techniques this version leaves on the table: sandwich capacitors, the
comparator VALID signal, and cross-coupled capacitors in the S/H. Layout and component mismatch were
also out of scope, so the numbers here are schematic-level. The most instructive part of the project
was not any single block — each of them simulated correctly in isolation long before the assembled
converter did. Getting a working ADC meant understanding how the blocks load and time each other,
and that is where most of the effort went.
